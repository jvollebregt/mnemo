# MNEMO lessons: first drafts

*To rewrite together with Joos, with Mary Oliver's "A Poetry Handbook" (1994) and "Rules for the Dance" (1998) at hand. Both are in copyright, so lessons are written in our own words in her spirit, with short attributed quotes at most. Each lesson opens once, from the bottom of the page, the first time its pencil tool is picked. Keep each lesson to about 120 words plus one "try it" line.*

## Beats
Say the line aloud, the way you would say it to a friend, and listen for the syllables your voice leans on: HOPE is the THING with FEA-thers. Those are the beats.
A trick (Joos): say it again with your mouth shut, just humming. The words disappear and the pulse is left: DUM da da DUM da DUM da.
Words have shapes of their own. FEA-thers goes DUM-da; with-OUT goes da-DUM. Lines are built from these little shapes, and they come back.
There is no wrong answer here. Mark what you hear. Later, when the words have faded, the beats will tell you how long the line is and where its weight falls.
*Try it: tap the syllables your voice leans on. When you move on to the next line, the light syllables of the last one get their own small mark, so you can count them too.*

### More about beats (optional)
When a poem keeps returning to one shape, it has a meter. Poets gave the shapes names: da-DUM is an iamb, DUM-da a trochee, da-da-DUM an anapest.
Dickinson writes mostly in da-DUM, four beats in one line and three in the next. It is the meter of the hymns she sang in church, called common meter, which is why her poems can be sung to *Amazing Grace*.
Meter is a pattern the ear expects, not a rule the poet obeys. The interesting moments are where she breaks it: listen for them.
*Try it: hum the first two lines and count the beats in each.*

## Echoes (tool name; covers rhyme, alliteration, assonance, consonance)
A poem is made of sounds as much as of words, and good poems make their sounds come back. Every sound that comes back is an echo, and every echo is a kind of rhyme.
Rhyme at the end of words is the one everybody knows: *heard* comes back in *Bird*. Rhyme at the front of words is alliteration, which is why it used to be called *head rhyme*: *sings, stops, soul*.
Echoes can hide inside words too: a vowel coming back (assonance), or a consonant (consonance). *Soul* and *all* only half-rhyme; Dickinson loved that slant chime.
Give each family of echoes its own kind of underline. At the end of a line an echo is what memory leans on most; when you recall the line, it tells you which word has to come.
*Try it: choose an underline, then tap* sings *and* stops. *For another sound, choose another underline.*

**Framing (Joos):** alliteration is presented as a form of rhyme. "Head rhyme" / "initial rhyme" is an established older term for alliteration. **To check in Oliver's *A Poetry Handbook*** (Joos has the book): does she frame the sound devices as a spectrum with rhyme, and is there a phrase we can quote briefly with attribution?

## Pauses (the third tool)
*Marked pauses survive the fade; unmarked punctuation fades with the words. In the app, pauses can also be marked where there is no punctuation (a breath between words).*
Punctuation is where the voice rests or turns. A comma is a short rest, a full stop an ending. Dickinson's dashes are breaths held mid-thought.
A pause in the middle of a line is a caesura; a line that ends on punctuation is end-stopped, and one that runs on into the next is enjambed.
Ink the stops. When the words have faded, they still show you where the line breathes.
*Try it: tap the dashes in the first line.*
